## Chapter 2 Highlights

# Seven Stages of Action
>_"Most innovation is done as an incremental enhancement of existing products." - Ch.2, pg.43_

![Smartphone Timeline](https://encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com/images?q=tbn:ANd9GcQVYlaSekCDiVDoqtn0UYRNQp1HHJ7Bm3lxW96eHYh1pNF1Zw_Z4n25TQE&s=10](https://azure-na-images.contentstack.com/v3/assets/blt1ab210a573e530eb/blt6a41f375f640812a/690d22ed42faa9dd06595d0e/History_Blog_Hero_1.png?auto=webp)](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/69b40ac1991ba644da780c5a/6a33e9b3bbac13f4aa073691_smartphone-history-when-were-smartphones-invented_1.webp))

The entire Seven Stages of Action section was an interesting read. It is a thought process that has come across my mind a few times personally before reading this material. The section made me think of the development of smartphones, and how the stages of action are displayed within their design. The planning, to execution, to observation phases were initially very poor with the first ever smartphone, the IBM Simon Personal Communicator. It was a clunky device that loosely resembles what people think of today when hearing the word "smartphone." The stages of action put into play were an abundant amount of observation and remedying execution, all to circle back to the execution phase to achieve the planned goal. The quote listed above struck a chord, as the incremental development of the smartphone since the IBM made this seven stage process much more intuitive. The first iPhone still had its own learning curve, but with easier ways to execute on plans (with the introduction of the touchscreen), with clearer labels and signifiers matching familiar mental models before the device's time. Since the first iPhone, the device has evolved to refine its use. Its evolution is spot-on incremental, based off of previous smartphones as well as competitor devices.

# Human Cognition and Emotion
>_"For designers, the most critical aspect of the behavioral level is that every action is associated with an expectation." Ch.2, pg.52_

![PS3 Controller]([https://encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com/images?q=tbn:ANd9GcTQog17Cx5QkFoP9zbgPw008HqZqwQQS4GkJnEZw_UjjiZLeUUkdUizbgpD&s=10](https://i5.walmartimages.com/asr/ec6aff92-015d-41cb-b4bc-96c11a681673.d27f2dd2b79643ba2df7c698090390e6.jpeg))

Affordance and signals is a very important principle when talking about design. An object or system is only as good as the user knows how to use  it. If there is no indication of what an object might be capable of, or documentation of what a system offers and how to properly use it, it can render objects, functions, and sometimes entire products useless. For myself, I instantly thought of the small pocket in jeans. To this day, no one really knows what it is used for! You _can_ put pocket change in there, but there are much better alternatives that most people carry around everyday (usually inside their bigger pockets!). As far as design goes, there is no strong perceivable signifier as to what this pocket affords a user. Naturally, most do not use this small pocket at all. This tiny pocket is rendered useless in most people's lives, all because there is no easy-to-see signal of what it should be used for.
