## Chapter 2 Highlights

# Seven Stages of Action
>_"Most innovation is done as an incremental enhancement of existing products." - Ch.2, pg.43_

![Smartphone Timeline](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/69b40ac1991ba644da780c5a/6a33e9b3bbac13f4aa073691_smartphone-history-when-were-smartphones-invented_1.webp)

The entire Seven Stages of Action section was an interesting read. It is a thought process that has come across my mind a few times personally before reading this material. The section made me think of the development of smartphones, and how the stages of action are displayed within their design. The planning, to execution, to observation phases were initially very poor with the first ever smartphone, the IBM Simon Personal Communicator. It was a clunky device that loosely resembles what people think of today when hearing the word "smartphone." The stages of action put into play were an abundant amount of observation and remedying execution, all to circle back to the execution phase to achieve the planned goal. The quote listed above struck a chord, as the incremental development of the smartphone since the IBM made this seven stage process much more intuitive. The first iPhone still had its own learning curve, but with easier ways to execute on plans (with the introduction of the touchscreen), with clearer labels and signifiers matching familiar mental models before the device's time. Since the first iPhone, the device has evolved to refine its use. Its evolution is spot-on incremental, based off of previous smartphones as well as competitor devices.

# Human Cognition and Emotion
>_"For designers, the most critical aspect of the behavioral level is that every action is associated with an expectation." Ch.2, pg.52_

![PS3 Controller](https://i5.walmartimages.com/seo/For-SONY-PS3-Controller-Support-Bluetooth-Wireless-Gamepad-for-Play-Station-3-Joystick-Console-for-PS3-Controle-For-PC-Black-New_910e8f52-07f4-444b-8387-796d2ae5564b.a24bec4b6fe79f76cf682f12127a5b8d.png)

This clause made me think of game controllers for video game consoles. Every button on a game controller is associated with an action. Even though games differ, the framework by which games map out the controller's actions, and the buttons that execute those actions, are largely standardized. For example, the bottom button on the right-side button pad (commonly "X" or "A") is largely used to jump in video games. Thus, most players uphold the expectation that the this button is used to jump in video games. Players that experience a game with a different mapping for jumping, say on the top button (commonly "▲" or "Y") will get confused. The game's controls will become unintuitive. Even with labels and signals disclosing what button executes what action, an unfamiliar mapping could cause players friction when playing the game, and could lead to them never playing again!
